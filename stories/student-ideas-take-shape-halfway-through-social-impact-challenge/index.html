Student ideas take shape halfway through the Social Impact Challenge

Halfway through Boise State’s Social Impact Challenge, students are refining their projects ahead of the final pitch meeting, with up to $3,000 going to the winning project.

By Jack McElwee · The Arbiter

Halfway through Boise State’s Social Impact Challenge, students are refining their projects ahead of the final pitch meeting on Nov. 6.

As previously reported by The Arbiter, the Social Impact Challenge encourages students to explore ideas addressing community problems, with the winning project receiving up to $3,000 in prize money.

“It’s going quite well,” said Mike Stefancic, director of student success for the public service unit. “Students are really excited and they’re motivated to do these types of projects, and that’s really what the Social Impact Challenge is about – just giving them space and some support to take their ideas and incentivize them to do that.”

Students are beginning to formulate their ideas while preparing their presentations for the final pitch.

Senior Charlotte McNeely is working on a plan to raise awareness and support legislation that would ban anonymous social media apps such as Yik Yak from being used on school Wi-Fi.

Although she graduates in May, McNeely hopes her project helps prevent people from becoming desensitized to rude behavior online.

“I think that as social media and the use of technology and AI become so much more relevant in our today’s society, that people don’t necessarily always think about, ‘oh, there’s a human on the other side of my comment,’ and it hurts people,” McNeely said.” And just trying to change that mentality from thinking that it’s okay to be nasty online, kind of thing.”

Freshman Chloe Mann is also narrowing down her focus for the project. She hopes to create an app that connects people with nonprofit food banks.

“I’m really focusing on the shame that comes with it,” Mann said. “One of my ideas was to do an app where people could post anonymously about their struggles without feeling embarrassed about it.”

Mann said she would need to outsource the app-building portion of her project if she wins the competition, as she does not have a background in software development.

While students can receive academic credit for participating in the challenge, that is not the only benefit.

“I’ve heard that from numerous other students, whether they took their idea on or not, they use this experience in job interviews, on their resume, and things like that, and had directly received feedback from employers that that stood out to them too as well,” Stefancic said.

Stefancic hopes the students find future success through the opportunities this project opens for them.

“I think the biggest goal is that this becomes a beginning for them,” Stefancic said. “ I want to see their ideas continue on, whether that is the [project] itself or a version of that that kind of moves on.”
