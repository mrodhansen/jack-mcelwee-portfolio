$8 million dollar federal grant addresses Idaho teacher shortage

Boise State University’s College of Education is using an $8 million federal grant to offer paid, full-time teaching positions and help new teachers stay in Idaho schools.

By Jack McElwee · The Arbiter

Boise State University’s College of Education is using an $8 million federal grant to offer paid, full-time teaching positions and help new teachers stay in Idaho schools.

The university received the Teacher Quality Partnership grant from the U.S. Department of Education to fund Project TRAIL (Teacher Residency Academy for Induction and Leadership).

“One of the exciting outcomes of the grant is that we’ll be working with nine rural districts to be able to place student teachers… in those districts after they graduate,” said Dr. Hannah Carter, principal investigator of Project TRAIL.

Parma, Kuna and Caldwell are among the participating districts and they will hire graduates of Project TRAIL.

To be considered for the initiative, student teachers must first be accepted into either the elementary, second education or special education master’s programs in the College of Education.

Once accepted, the students must submit written and video essays to find out if they have a disposition that lends itself to teaching.

“It’s really important in teaching to have a reflective inquiry mindset and curiosity, wanting to be coached and wanting to learn,” said Dr. Megan Lynch, the project’s co-principal investigator. “We’re also looking at a few characteristics like (if they are) from the area.”

Student teachers typically complete a “professional year” in the final year of their master’s program. Project TRAIL will turn this usually unpaid year into a paid, full-time residency in local schools.

The project will then help transition teachers from the professional year, into a formal teaching position.

“If you are from the Kuna School District and that’s where you want to return to those students, that’s going to weigh heavier on that overall application,” Lynch siad.

A key goal of the initiative is to give new teachers what Lynch calls “induction support” after graduation.

“We call those first few years of teaching between year one to year five as your induction into teaching,” Lynch said. “If you’re left on your own, the research shows you have a greater chance of being burnt out, not having support and leaving the teaching profession.”

Induction support reduces burnout by providing a cohort where new teachers can come together to discuss challenges they face early in their careers. They will also have opportunities to take graduate courses in areas they want to explore further.

“The grant will provide the stipends for graduate courses that they can take together to continue to develop in those first few years of teaching,” Lynch said.

In the three years Project TRAIL will run 75 teachers through the program in the resident, early-career as well as the mentor and teacher leader categories.

Every year in Idaho, around 2,000 teaching vacancies must be filled, according to a 2023 report from the Idaho State Board of Education. Lynch hopes the more experienced graduating teachers will foster a culture in their schools that encourages more teachers to stay.

“You don’t need to replace 2,000 teachers if they’re not leaving,” Lynch said.

Lynch said the initiative gives the College of Education time to conduct classroom research and identify ways to better support teachers.

“Part of this project is going to help us at the university get better at partnering with schools and partnering with teachers to identify what are the needs of first-year teachers,” Lynch said. “We’re preparing them based on what we know from scholarship, and what we know from experience.”
